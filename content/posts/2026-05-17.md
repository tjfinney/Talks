## In the beginning God

The first reading for today is the Hebrew account of creation. It begins like this:

> בְּרֵאשִׁ֖ית בָּרָ֣א אֱלֹהִ֑ים אֵ֥ת הַשָּׁמַ֖יִם וְאֵ֥ת הָאָֽרֶץ׃

> וְהָאָ֗רֶץ הָיְתָ֥ה תֹ֨הוּ֙ וָבֹ֔הוּ וְחֹ֖שֶׁךְ עַל־פְּנֵ֣י תְהֹ֑ום וְר֣וּחַ אֱלֹהִ֔ים מְרַחֶ֖פֶת עַל־פְּנֵ֥י הַמָּֽיִם׃ 

> וַיֹּ֥אמֶר אֱלֹהִ֖ים יְהִ֣י אֹ֑ור וַֽיְהִי־אֹֽור׃

[Gen 1.1-3 Hebrew Bible](https://marble.bible/text?s=undefined&dictid=000000&db=Hebrew&verse=1&textid=00100100200008&book=Genesis&chapter=1)

> Bereshith bara Elohim et ha shamayim ve'et ha'aretz

> Veha'aretz hayetah tohu vavohu ve'choshech al-penei tehom veruach Elohim merachefet al-penei hamayim

> Vayomer Elohim yehi or vayhi or

Sorry for my sorry attempt to pronounce it. Here is how the Jerusalem Bible translates it:

> In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.

> The earth was a formless void, there was darkness over the deep, and God's spirit hovered over the water.

> God said, 'Let there be light', and there was light.

In English you add *s* to a word to make it plural. In Hebrew you add *im*, and the word for God used here has the plural ending: *Elohim*. It seems that there is some aspect of plurality in the Creator: He is already there when creation begins, his spirit is there hovering over the water like a bird hovering over its young. His speech is there too, and what he says happens.

The [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint) is a translation of the Hebrew Bible into [Koine Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek) begun about 250 BC. *Septuagint* means *seventy*. Tradition says that seventy Hebrews did the translation. People refer to it using the abbreviation *LXX*, which is *seventy* in Roman numerals. Here is the beginning of Genesis in the Septuagint:

> ἐν ἀρχῇ ἐποίησεν ὁ θεὸς τὸν οὐρανὸν καὶ τὴν γῆν

[Gen 1.1 LXX](https://www.crosswire.org/study/parallelstudy.jsp?del=all&add=KJV&add=WLC&add=NASB&add=LXX)

John's Gospel begins with the same two words:

> εν αρχη ην ο λογος και ο λογος ην προς τον θεον και θεος ην ο λογος

In the beginning was the *logos*, and the *logos* was with God, and the *logos* *was* God.

What does that *logos* word mean? Here's a definition from Barclay Newman's *Concise Greek-English Dictionary of the New Testament*:

> something said (e.g. word; saying; message; teaching; talk, conversation; ... preaching); Word (in Johannine Christology); account, settlement of an account; value; reason, grounds; matter, thing; book.

As you can see, *logos* is a loaded word. According to John, God's Word was with God in the beginning, and in fact *is* God. As much as your speech is you, God's speech is God. John's message to us is that this Divine Reason for Being, God's voice through whom all things were created, became a human being and pitched his tent with us.

The Spirit was also with God in the beginning. In Hebrew the word is *ruach* and in Greek it is *pneuma*. Both words can mean *spirit*, or *breath*, or *wind*. Just as the Word is God, so is the Spirit. God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Spirit are as closely connected as your voice is connected to your breath. The Spirit hovers over creation like a bird protecting its young. The Spirit was with God in the beginning, a presence who is present in all that God does.

Early Christian theologians came up with a special word to describe the union of Father, Son, and Spirit. [Perichoresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perichoresis) means something like *co-inherence*. Augustine describes the union like this:

> In that highest Trinity one is as much as the three together, nor are two anything more than one. And They are infinite in themselves. So both each are in each, and all in each, and each in all, and all in all, and all are one. 

## Back to Genesis

Getting back to Genesis,

* God speaks to separate light from darkness: there is evening and morning, the first day. 

* God speaks to separate waters above from waters below: there is evening and morning, the second day. 

* God speaks to separate sea and land, telling the earth to produce plants: there is evening and morning, the third day.

* God speaks to make lights appear in the sky and shine on the earth: there is evening and morning, the fourth day.

* God speaks to make the sea teem with life the air to be filled with things that fly: there is evening and morning, the fifth day.

* God speaks to make life fill the land. Then God says, *Let us make humanity in our own image. And so God creates humanity, male and female he creates them. He blesses them and gives them the job of looking after his creation. There is evening and morning, the sixth day.

That is how heaven and earth were created with all their array. On the seventh day God completed his work. He blessed the seventh day and made it holy because on that day he rested from his creative work. That's where we get the Sabbath from. *Seven* in Hebrew is *Shabbat*. It's a good and God-given thing to rest on the seventh day.

## That's wrong isn't it?

So that's how the heavens and earth were made. But isn't that all wrong? Wasn't there a big bang that made all the hydrogen and a bit of helium? Wasn't there a first generation of stars that made the elements up to iron? Weren't there neutron star collisions and other cosmic conflagrations to make everything heavier than iron, including gold and uranium? Isn't that what our solar system formed from? Didn't it take thousands of millions of years to happen, rather than seven days? What about the sun, moon, and stars appearing after sea, sky, and land. Isn't this just a bronze age story with no relation to what really happened?

Recently I attended a [great presentation](https://iscast.org/events/iscast-perth-has-science-accidentally-found-evidence-there-is-a-god/) by Martin Hill. If you ever get a chance to see one of his presentations, I recommend it. His talk was titled *Has science accidentally found evidence there is a God.* He presented an array of recent scientific findings that are consistent with the Genesis account, including that the early earth was a dark water world. What frame of reference you use is always important in science. If your frame of reference is between the water below and the thick condensate above then the sun, moon, and stars do appear after the land and sea separate. As for the seven days, if I start talking about what happened *in my day*, you know I'm talking about an undefined expanse of time, as will be the length of today's message.

There is another aspect of the Genesis account that is worth considering. That is its poetic nature. The first day is about light and darkness. The second day is about sea and sky. The third day is about land and plants. The fourth day corresponds to the first day: it relates to lights in the sky. The fifth day parallels the second day: it has to do with fish in the sea and birds in the sky. The sixth day lines up with the third day: it talks about things that live on the land and humans to look after the land.

The poetic structure serves to emphasize the seventh day. This is a special day with no parallel, though it does hark back to the very beginning where all that was *was* God. God, who was already there before He spoke what we see into being, blesses the day and makes it holy. It is a day set apart for resting from work.
