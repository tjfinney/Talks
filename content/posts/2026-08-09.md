## Chiastic structure

Near the start of Noah's story it says:

> [The LORD] saw that the wickedness of man was great on the earth, and that the thoughts in his heart fashioned nothing but wickedness all day long.

[Gen 6.5 JB]

Near the end God says in his heart:

> Never again will I curse the earth because of man, because his heart contrives evil from his infancy.

[Gen 8.21 JB]

Did you notice a similarity?

Here's a Greek letter:

![Greek letter chi](/images/Chi.jpg)

There are 24 letters in the Greek alphabet. This is the 22nd. How is it pronounced? I'll go with *chi* as in *free* rather than *chi* as in *fry*.

There is a thing called [chiastic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiastic_structure) in the Bible. Some people call it ring structure. When something has this arrangement, the first and last parts match, the second part matches the second last, and so on all the way to the middle. It is a literary device used by the ancients to give hearers an idea of what is being emphasised, and that is often what is found in the centre.

## Chiastic structure in the flood account?

Some people think that the flood account has chiastic structure. Here is how [Gordon Wenham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Wenham) lays it out:

```
A: Noah and his sons (Gen 6:10)
    B: All life on earth (6:13:a)
        C: Curse on earth (6:13:b)
            D: Flood announced (6:7)
                E: Ark (6:14-16)
                    F: All living creatures (6:17–20 )
                        G: Food (6:21)
                            H: Animals in man's hands (7:2–3)
                                I: Entering the Ark (7:13–16)
                                    J: Waters increase (7:17–20)
                                        X: God remembers Noah (8:1)
                                    J': Waters decrease (8:13–14)
                                I': Exiting the Ark (8:15–19)
                            H': Animals (9:2,3)
                        G': Food (9:3,4)
                    F': All living creatures (9:10a)
                E': Ark (9:10b)
            D': No flood in future (9:11)
        C': Blessing on earth (9:12–17)
    B': All life on earth (9:16)
A': Noah and his sons (9:18,19a)
```

If this is right then the central part, the thing being emphasized is Genesis 8.1:

> But God remembered Noah and all the wild animals and the livestock that were with him in the ark, and he sent a wind over the earth, and the waters receded.

Can you think of any other parts in the Bible where God shows concern for the animals as well as the people? I think of where God talks to Jonah, who is in a very bad mood:

> You are only upset about a castor-oil plant which cost you no labour, which you did not make grow, which sprouted in a night and has perished in a night. And am I not to feel sorry for Nineveh, the great city, in which there are more than a hundred and twenty thousand people who cannot tell their right hand from their left, to say nothing of all the animals?

[Jonah 4.10-11 JB]

## Chiastic structure in 1 Peter?

Turning now to the reading we heard from 1 Peter, I imagine that you haven't heard many sermons on it. That might be due to the difficulties raised by the topic of preaching to the dead. Yet there it is.

What are we to make of this passage? Here is something that might help. It might be that there is chiastic structure here too. I've rearranged things so that parallels are brought together.

```
A (3.18-19):
Christ suffered, right-doer for wrong-doer, to bring you to God.
Put to death in the body, brought to life in the spirit.
Proclamation to imprisoned spirits.

A' (4.5-6): 
They are accountable to the One ready to judge the living and the dead. 
Good news preached to the dead.
Though judged as human beings in this life, yet they might live as God does in the spiritual realm.

B (3.20)
They were disobedient in Noah's day while God patiently waited.
Some were brought safely through water.

B' (4.3-4)
The past was long enough for wrong-doing.
They abuse you for no longer indulging in their flood of excesses.

C (3.21)
[The flood is] a foreshadowing of baptism that saves.
Not removal of physical dirt but an appeal to God for a clear conscience through the resurrection of Jesus Christ.

C' (4.1-2)
Since Christ suffered as a human, arm yourselves with the same mindset:
One who suffers in the body has quit sin.
Live your remaining time for what God wants, not human passions.

D (3.22):
Jesus Christ is on God's right: angels, authorities, and powers are subject to him.
```

I could easily be wrong about this arrangement, but if this is chiastic and if this is the right arrangement then the central point, the thing emphasized, is the part marked with 'D'.

## A quick detour

Now for a detour. I wonder if any of you are wondering how Peter got so good at literary niceties like chiastic structure? Wasn't he a Galilean fisherman? When did he get a classical education, something that usually required a family with means and a Greek context?

In those times a good Greek education focused on philosophy and rhetoric. Philosophy was about the reason for everything--a bit like science today. Rhetoric was about something just as important--how to persuade people. Back then it was all about good speaking. You would introduce yourself and say why people should listen to you. If you lost them, it was in the first minute or so because people would decide that what you are saying isn't worth the wear and tear on their ears.

A good speech was well structured. You would introduce yourself then state your topic--that which you would proceed to demonstrate. You would then address the topic piece by piece--thesis, antithesis, synthesis. All through you would use rhetorical devices, a whole screed of them, one of which was chiasmus. This helped the listeners know where you were placing the emphasis.

So where did Peter's rhetorical flair come from? There's a clue at the end of 1 Peter:

> I've written these few things to you through Silvanus (whom I regard as a trustworthy brother), urging and testifying that this is God's authentic grace, on which you are to stand firm.

[1 Peter 5.12 USC]

That's the Silvanus who is also known as Silas. The Book of Acts introduces him as a prophet, chosen to accompany Paul and Barnabas with the letter sent from the church in Jerusalem to the church in Antioch about what non-Jews had to do when they became Christians. (See [Acts 15.22-32](https://www.crosswire.org/study/parallelstudy.jsp?key=Acts+15%3A22#cv)). I think of 1 Peter as Peter's thoughts with Silas's polish. As another aside, there are similarities between 1 Peter and Hebrews, kindred themes and a plethora of literary devices. I wonder whether Silas wrote Hebrews?

## The central point

Getting back to the central point of today's reading from 1 Peter:

> Jesus Christ is on God's right: angels, authorities, and powers are subject to him.

I suppose one way to look at this is that nothing and no-one is beyond Christ's reach. He is the Messiah, the Christ, the Anointed One, God's appointed Judge, The One through whom all things were made, and whose powerful word sustains all things. He is at the beginning and at the end. He cannot be confounded. No power is able to frustrate him, even the power of death. He has cleared a way for the unclean to become clean. In fact, he *is* the way.

That doesn't make the other parts of this passage from 1 Peter worthless--they matter too. Christ suffered to bring you to God. In the same way we might have to go through a bit of pain to bring other people to God. That pain might be self-restraint, shedding the things we know ain't great. It might mean a bit of going out instead of staying in. I hear your pain! I don't like preaching stuff that I don't do myself, and here I am doing just that.

There is something else here that also matters:

> An appeal to God *for* a good conscience through the resurrection of Christ.

I am a human being and part of the human condition is to do the wrong thing all the time. I don't need you to tell me that my heart has contrived evil from my infancy because I know that myself. Even today my heart fashions nothing but wickedness all day long. What am I to do? How do I get a good conscience. How do I straighten up and fly right?

One thing I know is that I don't have the power to do it myself. I am crooked and any of my efforts to make my crookedness straight end up crooked. Maybe crooked in a different way but still crooked.

No, the power to become a right-doer instead of a wrong-doer has to come from somewhere else. The unanimous verdict of the New Testament writers is that this power is the same power that brought Jesus back from the dead. The power required to live right and have a good conscience is the resurrection of Christ. All those wrong things in the past are done, and Christ has cancelled their power by his sacrifice. He lives, not just to break the hold that the past has over us, but to give us the power to walk into a bright future. It's about relying on his resurrection power.

I think that's what Jesus is talking about when he says *Give us today our daily bread.* I think he's talking about the manna, the supernatural bread which God feeds his people. Jesus said, *I am the Bread of Life.* He is the bread from heaven. He lives by resurrection power and nothing can resist him, even death. Through his good life we can also live good lives.

Amen
