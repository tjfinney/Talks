A good reference

Something I have found out by studying too much is that a good reference is a good thing. Here’s a good reference on global warming and something called sustainability: Why care for the Environment? by John Houghton. According to Wikipedia,

Sir John Theodore Houghton CBE FRS FLSW (30 December 1931 – 15 April 2020) was a Welsh atmospheric physicist who was the co-chair of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s (IPCC) scientific assessment working group which shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2007 with Al Gore. He was lead editor of the first three IPCC reports. He was professor in atmospheric physics at the University of Oxford, former Director General at the Met Office and founder of the Hadley Centre.

If you’re wondering what the IPCC is, here is Wikipedia again:

The IPCC informs governments about the state of knowledge of climate change. It does this by examining all the relevant scientific literature on the subject. This includes the natural, economic and social impacts and risks. It also covers possible response options. The IPCC does not conduct its own original research. It aims to be objective and comprehensive. Thousands of scientists and other experts volunteer to review the publications. They compile key findings into “Assessment Reports” for policymakers and the general public; Experts have described this work as the biggest peer review process in the scientific community.

Getting back to John Houghton, he was a Christian man. Here’s something he said once:

If we want a good environmental policy in the future we’ll have to have a disaster. It’s like safety on public transport. The only way humans will act is if there’s been an accident.

The problem is, the environmental accident we’re heading for will be fatal for more and more of us. Given the high stakes, it would be wise to act now to try to stop the accident happening. More on that later. For now, here’s a quick summary of the article by John Houghton.

A quick summary of the good reference

Here’s a quick summary of the good reference taken from itself:

Global threats to the environment demand global solutions and sustainability provides the key. This [article] surveys this challenge with particular reference to Global Warming, describing the perils of inaction and some strategies for addressing the problem. Those who believe in God as creator and sustainer have a powerful motivation to care for God’s earth, and to take action on behalf of the poor, those who suffer most from environmental degradation.

Now, here’s a longer summary. John Houghton starts off by saying:

Imagine you are a member of the crew of a large space ship on a voyage to visit a distant planet. Your journey there and back will take many years. An adequate, high quality, source of energy is readily available in the radiation from the sun. Otherwise, resources for the journey are limited. The crew on the spacecraft is engaged for much of the time in managing the resources as carefully as possible. A local biosphere is created in the spacecraft where plants are grown for food and everything is recycled. Careful accounts are kept of all resources, with especial emphasis on non- replaceable components. That the resources be sustainable at least for the duration of the voyage, both there and back, is clearly essential.

This gives us an idea of what sustainability is about and why it is important. Lately, however, there has been a crisis of sustainability.

In 2009 a group of scientists led by Johan Rockström suggested that there were nine “planetary boundaries”, life support systems for humanity. These were: climate change; biodiversity loss; the biogeochemical cycles of nitrogen and phosphorus; ocean acidification; land use; freshwater; ozone depletion; atmospheric aerosols; and chemical pollution. Worryingly, it was estimated that the boundaries for climate change, biodiversity loss and nitrogen cycling had already been crossed, while others were likely to be crossed soon.

Concerning global warming,

Since the beginning of the industrial revolution around 1750, one of these greenhouse gases, carbon dioxide, has increased by over 40% and in July 2018 [was] at a higher concentration in the atmosphere (411 ppm) than for many hundreds of thousands of years. Chemical analysis demonstrates that this increase is due largely to the burning of fossil fuels – coal, oil and gas. If no action is taken to curb these emissions, the carbon dioxide concentration will rise during the twenty- first century to two or three times its pre-industrial level.

Over the twenty-first century the global average temperature is projected to rise by between 1.5 and 4 ºC from its pre-industrial level; the range represents different assumptions about greenhouse gas emissions and the sensitivity of the climate. For global average temperature, a rise of this amount is large. The difference between the middle of an ice age and the warm periods in between is only about 5 or 6 ºC. So, associated with likely warming in the twenty-first century will be a rate of change of climate equivalent to say, half an ice age in less than a hundred years – a larger rate of change than for at least 10,000 years. Adapting to this will be difficult for both humans and many ecosystems.

Concerning expected impacts, most will be adverse. They will include rising sea levels causing mass migrations, extreme weather events, and loss of polar ice sheets.

He goes on to say that there are three sorts of things that can be done to reduce global warming: better energy efficiency, use of non-fossil fuel sources, and sequestering carbon by planting forests or putting it back underground.

To finish, John Houghton talks about Stewards of Creation, New Attitudes, and Partnership with God.

On stewardship he says,

People often say to me that I am wasting my time talking about environmental sustainability. ‘The world’ they say ‘will never agree to take the necessary action.’ I reply that I am optimistic. One reason I give is that I believe that God is committed to His creation and that we have a God-given task of being good stewards of creation.

On new attitudes he says,

sustainability will never be achieved without a great deal more sharing. Sharing is an important Christian principle. John the Baptist preached about sharing (Luke 3:11), Jesus talked about sharing (Luke 12:33), the early church were prepared to share everything (Acts 4:32) and Paul advocated it (2 Cor. 8:13-15). The opposite of sharing – greed and covetousness – is condemned throughout scripture. At the individual level, a lot of sharing often occurs. At the international level it occurs much less as is well illustrated by the most condemning of world statistics – that the average flow of wealth in the world is from the poor to the rich.

On partnership with God, he finishes his article with these words:

An unmistakeable challenge is presented to the world-wide Christian church to take on the God- given responsibility of caring for the environment. It provides an unprecedented mission opportunity for Christians to take a lead and demonstrate love for God the world’s creator and redeemer, and love for our neighbours wherever they may be – remembering the words of Jesus, ‘From everyone who has been given much, much will be demanded’ (Luke 12: 48).

Hope in the face of hopelessness

So much for John Houghton’s article. I recommend it. Moving on from what it says, I’d like to finish up by talking about hope.

You have probably heard all about global warming before. These days there is so much bad news all the time that there is a strong need for good news. All the talk of disasters has made many of us disengage. I avoid the news these days and do other things instead, like play computer games or watch YouTubes about gold prospecting and other distractions. There is an overload of hopelessness and an underload of hopefulness. What can we do about this? Do you have any suggestions?

Here are a few things that make me more hopeful. There are some real prospects for being able to do energy production in a non-fossil-fuel way; things like thorium reactors and fusion reactors are possibilities but even without those, sodium ion batteries combined with solar and wind power have the potential to significantly reduce our reliance on coal, oil, and gas in the next few decades. Sodium features in other ways as well. MIT have come up with a sodium-air fuel cell for use in electric airliners. Another out-there technical possibility is to use sodium bicarbonate aersol to reflect heat away from the earth and thereby counteract global warming. Here’s a YouTube video on the topic that I found while trying to distract myself. Putting sodium in jet fuel could do the trick. (What could possibly go wrong with mixing liquid sodium and jet fuel? Watch the YouTube to see why it’s not at all a bad idea.)

Better than that, there is God. The whole Bible is full of hopeless situations that get turned around by God. God cares about us and the other things he made. He proves to be trustworthy over and over again. Jesus is God’s Son, and by connecting with him we become his agents of hope.
