God with us

November 1, 2026

Readings for today

The lectionary readings for today are from Isaiah 64, Psalm 80, 1 Corinthians 1, and Mark 13.

Isaiah 64

Isaiah was a prophet, as in a person who says what God tells them to say. His book starts with these words:

The vision of Isaiah the son of Amoz concerning Judah and Jerusalem, which he saw during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah.

Isaiah 1.1 NASB

Isaiah talks about someone he names Immanuel, meaning God with us:

For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace.

Isaiah 9.6 AV

Isaiah wrote that down seven centuries before Jesus of Nazareth was born.

Here is the part of Isaiah’s book appointed for reading today:

Oh, that you would tear the heavens open and come down–at your Presence the mountains would melt, as fire sets brushwood alight, as fire causes water to boil–to make known your name to your enemies, and make the nations tremble at your Presence, working unexpected miracles such as no one has ever heard of before. No ear has heard, no eye has seen any god but you act like this for those who trust him. You guide those who act with integrity and keep your ways in mind.

You were angry when we were sinners; we had long been rebels against you. We were all like men unclean, all that integrity of ours like filthy clothing. We have all withered like leaves and our sins blew us away like the wind. No one invoked your name or roused himself to catch hold of you. For you hid your face from us and gave us up to the power of our sins.

And yet, LORD, you are our Father; we the clay, you the potter, we are all the work of your hand.

Isaiah 64.1-8 JB

This was written when things were bad; Isaiah puts the devastation down to the people’s rebellion against God, and he counts himself among the rebels:

We have all withered like leaves and our sins blew us away like the wind. No one invoked your name or roused himself to catch hold of you. For you hid your face from us and gave us up to the power of our sins.

Despite the terrible circumstances, Isaiah still appeals to God;

And yet, LORD, you are our Father; we the clay, you the potter, we are all the work of your hand.

Isaiah says you are our Father. Once the disciples asked Jesus to teach them how to pray, and he began to teach them like this: Our Father. I used to wonder where Jesus got Our Father from, and this could be the place. This throws light on what Jesus means when he says Our Father. It is a way to approach God for people who feel unworthy of God’s blessing, a people who are sorely aware of their own failures, a people who are poor in spirit. It is for someone like Peter, who said Leave me master, for I am a sinful man after Jesus filled their nets with fish.

The amazing reality is that God is right there with them. God had indeed torn the heavens open and come down, but in a way they didn’t expect. Here is the Greek Mark uses to describe what happened when John baptized Jesus:

και ευθεως αναβαινων απο του υδατος ειδεν σχιζομενους τους ουρανους και το πνευμα ωσει περιστεραν καταβαινον επ αυτον

And straight away, coming up from the water, he saw the heavens tearing open and the Spirit coming down upon him like a dove.

[Mark 1.10]

Mark’s Gospel

Matthew, Mark and Luke have so many parallels that they are called the Synoptic Gospels; synoptic means to give an account from the same point of view or under the same general aspect A lot of people have spent a lot of time trying to work out how the synoptic gospels ended up like they are. A lot of these people think that Mark wrote first and that Matthew and Luke borrowed bits from him, though some would but Matthew first. Whatever happened, Mark’s Gospel is a wonderful book. Samuel has copies of Marks’ Gospel in the form of a little book called The Story of Jesus the Rescuer God. It is by the Bible Society and contains the Contemporary English Version of Mark’s Gospel. This is a great translation and I heartily recommend it.

As you may have feared, I cannot resist telling you more about the history of Mark’s Gospel. Eusebius wrote his [Church History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_History_(Eusebius) around 325 AD. In it he quotes something that [Papias of Hierapolis](Papias of Hierapolis) wrote around 110 AD:

Mark, indeed, having been the interpreter of Peter, wrote accurately, howbeit not in order, all that he recalled of what was either said or done by the Lord. For he neither heard the Lord, nor was he a follower of his, but, at a later date (as I said), of Peter; who used to adapt his instructions to the needs [of the moment], but not with a view to putting together the Dominical oracles in orderly fashion: so that Mark did no wrong in thus writing some things as he recalled them. For he kept a single aim in view: not to omit anything of what he heard, nor to state anything therein falsely.

[A New Eusebius, Ed. J. Stevenson, rev. W. H. C. Frend, London: SPCK, 1987]

There is a lot here that deserves comment and–as you are painfully aware–I could go on about it all day, but I will shorten it for your sake. This Mark is the John Mark mentioned in the Book of Acts. Peter went to his mother’s house after the Lord’s angel sprung him from jail (Acts 12). He was the one over whom Paul and Barnabas had such a sharp dispute that they parted ways (Acts 16). He is also the one who Peter calls my son (1 Peter 5.13). That is commonly taken to mean his spiritual son but if Peter is speaking literally then the disagreement between Paul and Barnabas was an argument over Peter’s actual son.

You might have noticed that Papias said howbeit not in order. That is often taken to mean that Mark’s Gospel does not give the events in time order. However the Greek word behind in order can also mean thematically. Thematic is a good description of the Gospels of Matthew and Luke where Jesus’s teaching on particular topics are gathered together into sections. Given this understanding of what Papias means, there is nothing to stop Mark’s Gospel being Peter’s running commentary on what happened from the time he met Jesus to when Jesus came back from the dead after being crucified in Jerusalem. In other words, Mark’s Gospel could give the events in time order.

Cosmic signs

That brings us to the Gospel reading for today. Today is the first day of Advent, and this reading is about the advent of Jesus Christ, when he returns to take his place as the rightful heir of all things.

To give the context, it was just before the Passover festival. One day as Jesus and his disciples were leaving the Temple one of them said,

Teacher, just look how large these stones are, and how extensive the buildings!

But Jesus replied,

Not one stone will be left on another.

Later, when they were sitting on the Mount of Olives opposite the Temple, Peter, James, John, and Andrew asked him about it:

When will these events take place, and what will be the sign that all these things are about to come to an end?

[Mark 13.1-4 USC]

Jesus goes on to talk about people claiming to be him, wars, earthquakes, famines, and religious persecution. When something called the abomination of desolation is set up where it should not be then people in Judea need to get out of there without delay. Jesus says that if anyone says Look, here is the Messiah! Look, there he is! don’t believe it because false Messiahs and false prophets will spring up and do miraculous signs and amazing deeds to deceive even God’s chosen, if that were possible. Then Jesus says,

You are to be on the lookout; I have told you everything in advance.

That was only the first half of Jesus’s answer. The second half is the reading for today, Mark 13.24-37:

But at that time, after that distress, the sun will go dark; and the moon won’t give her light; the stars will be falling out of the sky; and the forces of heaven will be shaken. At that time people will see the Son of Man arriving in clouds with great power and splendour. Then he will send the angels and gather those he’s chosen from the four points of the compass, from earth’s extremity to the sky’s extremity.

[Mark 13.24-27 USC]

What Jesus says here is a tapestry of scriptures found in Isaiah (13.10, 34.4), Ezekiel (32.7-8), Joel (2.10, 2.31, 3.15), Daniel (7.13-14), Zechariah (2.6, 2.10), and Deuteronomy (30.4).

Jesus says to learn a lesson from the fig tree: when it sprouts you know summer is near. In the same way, when you see these things, you will know that he is near, at the very gates. And then he says,

I am telling you a fact, that this generation won’t pass away until all these events take place.

[Mark 13.28-30 USC]

What does he mean by this generation? We know that the Temple was reduced to rubble by the Romans in 70 AD. Was Jesus wrong? I don’t think so. I think that this mob is a not too terrible translation. He could be talking about the political establishment, the religious establishment, the people around when the events he has just mentioned happen, or some other group.

One thing he makes clear is that no-one, not even he himself, knows exactly when it will be. Only our Father knows that.

Jesus then says:

Keep a lookout! Be on the alert! For you don’t know the time when it’s going to happen.

[Mark 13.33 USC]

He tells us that we need to stay awake so that we won’t be found sleeping on the job when he comes back. To finish he says,

What I’m telling you I’m telling everybody: stay wide awake!

[Mark 13.37 USC]

How do we do that?

What’s the best way to avoid sleeping on the job? In the Garden of Gethsemane the disciples kept falling asleep. Then Jesus said to Peter,

Are you asleep? Couldn’t you keep watch for one hour? You are all to keep watch, and pray that you won’t be put to the test. Your spirits are willing enough, but your human natures are weak.

[Mark 14.38 USC]

Prayer is important. There’s a line in one of Paul’s letters where he says,

Persevere in prayer, staying alert by means of it, with thanksgiving.

[Col 4.2 USC]

We are all different. Some of us have a gift in the area of prayer but others–like me–are not so gifted. What can I do? I will take a lead from Paul and persevere. I’ll keep at it. I’ll make time to talk, make time to walk with God. I’ll take time to think about what to be grateful for. I’ll take time to commune with the life-giving Spirit who hovers over the chaos. He will lead us by still waters, he will bring us to green fields, he will restore our souls.

Bring us back Mighty God, let your face smile on us and we shall be safe.

[Psalm 80.3, 7, 19]

Amen
